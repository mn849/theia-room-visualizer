2 · Motivation

Buying secondhand is clunky, cumbersome and frustrating

Facebook Marketplace is full of good, affordable furniture: a solid-wood bench for $60, a pair of antique side tables, a mirror nobody else has. For students, renters and anyone furnishing a first apartment, it's often the best place to shop. But buying there is far harder than it should be.

Listings are messy and inconsistent

Every listing is different. Some have measurements, many don't. When they do, they're written a dozen ways: "24 inch w", "20.5″W x 15″D x 28″H", "Seat height 18". The photos show the item against someone else's wall, in someone else's light. None of it tells you the one thing you need to know: will this look right, and fit, in my room?

So buyers guess, or give up

  1. Screenshot the listing and try to picture it at home.
  2. Pull out a tape measure and compare numbers in your head.
  3. Or spend 10–20 minutes cutting the item out in a photo editor and pasting it into a photo of your room, guessing the scale.
  4. Then repeat for every listing you're considering, while good listings sell within hours.

Getting it wrong is costly. Sales are usually pick-up only and final, so a piece that doesn't fit means renting a van, hauling it home, and reselling it at a loss. Many people simply don't take the risk and buy new instead.

And then you have to message a stranger

Even once you like something, you still have to write to the seller: ask if it's still available, ask for the measurement they left out, arrange a pick-up. It's a small task, but it takes mental energy, and that friction is enough to make people close the tab.

The goal: make buying secondhand less clunky, less cumbersome and less frustrating. If people can see a messy, inconsistent listing in their own space, at the right size, and send the seller a message in one click, more of them will buy secondhand.

3 · What is new here

Room visualisation for items that have no 3D model

Room visualisers exist, but they work on the retailer's own catalogue, where every product already has a professionally made 3D model. A Marketplace listing has two or three phone photos and maybe a line of text.

What Theia does differently

Theia works with the listings people actually find: one-off secondhand items with a few phone photos and inconsistent text. Here is what it does, feature by feature.

1 · Faithful cutouts

Theia removes the background from the seller's own photos and keeps every pixel of the item itself, including thin legs, spindles and patterned upholstery. Nothing is redrawn, and one click switches back to the untouched photo.

2 · Real sizes from messy listings

gpt-6-luna reads the whole listing and pulls out the seller's measurements in any format. Every number is checked against the listing text, so the model can't invent a size. If only one size is given, Theia works out the rest from the photo. If none is given, it estimates and says so. The user can always correct the height.

3 · Room sizing and perspective

From a single room photo, Theia works out where the camera was, the floor and the walls. Clicking four floor corners and entering one room measurement (width or length) makes it accurate. Items then stand on the floor at their true size, and get smaller as you drag them further back, just as they would in real life.

4 · 2D and 3D views

The quick 2D view places items straight onto your photo. The 3D view rebuilds the room and lets you look around and step through it. Items snap flush to walls, and pairs like bedside tables are split so each piece moves on its own.

5 · Message the seller in one click

Press ♡ Love it and a second agent drafts a short, natural first message: it asks if the item is still available, asks for any missing measurement, and offers to pick it up. You can edit it, copy it, and open the listing. Theia never sends anything for you.

6 · Compare up to 15 items, with history

Up to 15 items can sit in the room at once and stay where you put them. A history panel lists every listing you've tried this session, so you can bring back anything you removed.

Honest caveat: we compared against these products from their public descriptions and our own use, not a formal benchmark. Some of them do render better when a catalogue 3D model exists. Theia's advantage is coverage of one-off secondhand items and trustworthiness, not visual polish.

4 · The agents

Two narrow language agents and four deterministic tools

We deliberately use the language model for only one job: reading messy listing text and deciding what kind of object it is and how it sits (upright, flat like a rug, or on a wall). Everything that has to be exact (dimensions, pixels, geometry) is done by deterministic, testable code. The LLM can't overwrite any of it.

ComponentWhat it doesTools / inputs it may use
Listing agent (listing_agent.py)Classifies the item (bench, mirror, rug…), picks a pose, raises warnings ("description truncated", "ambiguous item"), and copies the seller's own stated measurements for one piece (e.g. 20.5″W × 15″D × 28″H).Only the delimited listing title (≤1,000 chars) and description (≤6,000 chars). No browsing, no image access, no write actions. Output is validated JSON; a measurement is accepted only if every number literally appears in the listing text, so the model cannot invent a size.

It can't send anything: the user edits, copies and sends. The reply is rejected (with a template fallback) if it isn't 2–4 sentences, is over 480 characters, or contains links, emails, phone numbers or placeholders.

Model choice

gpt-6-luna via Portkey (Responses API). The job is short classification of untrusted text, so a small, cheap, fast model is enough. It's also the only model allowed on the course key. Early development runs in our log used gpt-6-astra and gpt-6-sol; both agents (listing classifier and seller-message drafter) are now fixed to gpt-6-luna in code. Vision and geometry deliberately don't use an LLM. They run locally, cost no tokens and can't invent details.

Step 6: switch to 3D. The browser receives the camera, floor polygon, walls and items (in metres). The view opens at the photo's own viewpoint, so it first looks exactly like the photo, with the bench standing on open floor about 2.4 m away and facing you. You then walk through the room: drag to look around, scroll or W/S to walk, A/D to step sideways. Movement is kept within about 2 m of where the photo was taken, because a single photo can only be reprojected convincingly near its own viewpoint. Earlier versions had separate Photo, Orbit and Top views; testing showed one walk-through mode was easier to understand. The user can drag it, rotate it with Q/E and orbit around it.

4.2 How one photo becomes a 3D room

  1. Lens. The focal length comes from EXIF (35 mm-equivalent). If that's missing, it comes from the clicked corners: a rectangle's two vanishing directions must be perpendicular. If that fails too, we assume a typical 26 mm phone lens.
  2. Floor and camera. Four clicked corners give a homography from the floor rectangle to the image, and from it the floor's orientation and extent. Hand clicks are rarely a perfect rectangle, so when the photo has enough straight vertical edges we take the tilt and roll from those instead and use the clicks only for where the floor and walls are. Without clicks, we likewise find straight vertical edges (door frames, wall corners, furniture legs), robustly fit their common vanishing point (RANSAC), and get the camera's tilt and roll from it.
  3. Scale. Your confirmed room width if you entered one; otherwise a 1.45 m phone height. The app always tells you which it used.
  4. Room box. The far edge of the clicked floor is the back wall and the sides are the side walls, at the ceiling height you set. The photo is projected back onto these surfaces, so the opening view reproduces the original photo exactly.
  5. Depth (optional). A local depth network's relative depth is calibrated to metres using floor pixels whose true distance we know from step 2.
{{img:docs/figures/geometry_recovery.png|Test on a computer-generated room with a known camera: from the four orange clicks Theia recovers the camera height (1.40 m), tilt (−12.0°) and roll (1.5°) exactly. It then places a 1.2 m bench flush against the back wall and a 0.9 m chair against the right wall, in correct perspective. Before this work, items were flat cutouts pasted at a fixed screen position and resized by a rough depth rule. That flat placement is still available as the quick 2D mode.}}

At scale, model tokens are not the main cost. Hosting and storage are: a small always-on web instance plus CPU time for cutouts and depth inference, which run locally rather than through a paid vision API.

What we did to keep cost down

  • The LLM does one narrow job. It doesn't see images, extract dimensions or reason about geometry. Image-to-3D generators or vision models would cost far more per item and would also invent details.
  • Input caps: title ≤1,000 chars and description ≤6,000 chars (tested: a 20,147-character description is cut to 6,000).
  • Result cache: re-adding the same listing costs 0 tokens (tested: 5 identical adds give 1 model call). Our own log shows why this matters: of the 13 calls, the same bench listing was classified 10 times, with identical token counts and outputs. With the cache, about 8 of those 13 calls would have been free.
  • Hourly cap (default 60 calls/hour) bounds runaway loops (see §6).
  • Zero-token fallback when there is no key, a network error, or the cap is reached.

Did cost cutting hurt quality?

For classification, not in our runs: the same bench listing came back as "bench / upright" whether the model saw a 119-token input or the full 625-token one. The cost cut that did hurt was the old 800 px image cap, which was originally there to keep browser session storage small. It visibly erased detail from items, which was this project's main user complaint. We fixed it by moving images to a server-side cache, so photos are now kept at 1600 px (§6, §7).

7 · Impact

A trustworthy preview that made a real buyer more likely to purchase

Quality: the item now looks like the item

The most important problem we fixed is the one users noticed first: the old cutout pipeline stripped detail out of items. It downscaled photos twice to 800 px, forced soft edges to hard on/off, painted the wall into the gaps between spindles with a gap-filling filter, and its fallback erased thin legs. The new pipeline keeps 1600 px and soft edges, restores pale upholstery, and clears only regions that match the wall colour at the same height in the photo.

{{img:docs/figures/cutout_before_after.png|Same test photo through both pipelines. Before: wall visible between the spindles and under the seat is kept as part of the bench. After: gaps are transparent while the cream seat and thin legs are kept. (Generated test image so both pipelines see identical input; real listings behave the same way. See the user test below.)}}

On a real listing the difference is bigger still. General segmentation models (here U²-Net) give patterned upholstery a weak, "ghosted" alpha. Theia now uses the model only as a guide: confident pixels stay, the frame border is background, and GrabCut's colour models decide the uncertain pixels. GrabCut may only promote pixels where the model saw a real signal (alpha ≥ 0.09), so the wall seen through the bench, which the model scored about 0.04, stays transparent.

{{img:docs/figures/real_bench_cutout.png|The real Marketplace bench from our test run. Before: the tapestry seat and apron are almost transparent (the bug reported during testing). After: seat, apron, turned legs and stretcher are kept, and the wall and floor seen between the legs are removed.}}

What this means at scale

If Theia saves a buyer even one wrong pick-up per apartment hunt, that's roughly an hour of driving and carrying plus a resale loss, and it costs about two cents in model tokens per user per month. Our one test user said Theia made him more likely to buy on Marketplace, which is the behaviour change that matters to both buyers and sellers. The ♥ seller message also removes the first hurdle of contacting a seller.

8 · Limits and next steps

Where the prototype stops

  • One photo isn't a 3D scan. Without the depth model, existing furniture in the photo is painted flat onto the floor and walls when you move away from the photo viewpoint, which is why walking is limited to about 2 m. Without floor clicks, the layout is "rough" and the scale assumes a 1.45 m phone height.
  • Items are photo cards, not meshes. They look right from the angles the seller photographed, and show a silhouette elsewhere. That's honest, but not full 3D.
  • Cutouts can still fail when the item is the same colour as the wall behind it (for example a white table against a white wall). The original-photo toggle is the escape hatch.
  • Stitched panoramas are not a single pinhole camera, so 3D from a panorama is approximate. One photo works best.
  • Facebook hides the full description from logged-out requests. Theia never logs in, so it often receives only a shortened preview. For the side-table listing used in testing, the seller's "20.5″W × 15″D × 28″H" was cut off; Theia correctly labelled its size "estimated" (32 × 55 cm from the photo) rather than "seller-stated", and the user corrected it with Set size (71 cm). A browser extension that reads the listing from the user's own logged-in page is the fix.
  • Room scale from one photo is approximate. Without clicked floor corners and one confirmed room width or length, the camera height is assumed (1.45 m) and sizes can be off by tens of percent. Each item's card shows the size being used, and the user can set the real height.
  • three.js loads from a CDN, so the 3D view needs internet; offline, the 2D preview is used.

What live testing found (and fixed)

Testing with real listings in a real room exposed several bugs. Each one was traced from logs, not guessed, and fixed:

  • "Listed 1w ago" read as "1 inch wide". The size parser treated Facebook's timestamp as a measurement, so the tables were drawn about 2 cm wide. Timestamps, page code (width:100px) and impossible sizes for the item type are now ignored.
  • Large items pinned to the bottom edge. Keeping the top of an item on screen pushed its base to the bottom of the photo, so it was sized as if it stood right in front of the camera. The item's base on the floor is now its position.
  • Adding a listing removed earlier items. Re-drawing the item cards made each "Remove" button register as a click. Only real clicks now count, which made a 15-item room with a session history possible.
  • "Rug" detection matched "material". A birdcage was laid flat like a rug. Only whole words now count.
{{shot:bug-too-big|Before the fix: tables drawn far too large because their base was pinned to the bottom of the photo.}}{{shot:bug-too-small|Before the fix: tables drawn 1 inch wide after "Listed 1w ago" was misread as a size.}}

With another month

  1. Phone depth capture (Apple RoomPlan or LiDAR) for a measured room. The iOS scaffolding is already in the repo.
  2. A cutout touch-up brush with undo, and automatic angle tagging of gallery photos.
  3. Real occlusion and lighting matching, so placed items take on the room's light.
  4. A browser extension "Send to Theia" button on Marketplace pages, which would also read the full, logged-in listing text and its measurements.
  5. A five-person study measuring time-to-decision, wrong-pick-up rate and trust ratings against the photo-editor baseline.
Boundary: Theia helps decide whether an item is worth the trip. It doesn't replace a tape measure or confirming with the seller.
