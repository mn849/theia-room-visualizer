2 · Motivation

Secondhand furniture is cheap to buy and expensive to get wrong

Facebook Marketplace is where a lot of people furnish an apartment, especially students and people who move often. The listings are full of unusual and affordable pieces: spindle benches, mirrors, vintage chairs. The decision a buyer has to make is "will this look right and fit in my room?" The listing doesn't answer that. It shows the object against the seller's wall, often with no dimensions, or with dimensions buried in the description in an inconsistent format ("24 inch w", "24.5 h", "Seat height 18").

How people deal with it today

  1. Screenshot the listing and message the seller for measurements, then wait.
  2. Take a tape measure to the room and try to picture it.
  3. Or open a photo editor, cut the item out by hand, paste it into a room photo and resize it by eye. That takes 10–20 minutes per item and the scale is a guess.
  4. Repeat for every candidate listing, often under time pressure because good listings go fast.

What it costs

Getting it wrong is costly. Marketplace sales are usually final and pick-up only, so a piece that doesn't fit means renting a van, carrying it home, and then reselling it at a loss or abandoning it. Getting it right is slow: the buyer either waits on the seller or spends a long time in a photo editor for each item. The people most exposed are the ones Marketplace serves best: students, renters and first apartments, where budgets are tight and rooms are small.

Goal: in a few minutes, a first-time user can put several real listings into their own room, at the right scale and perspective, look at them from different angles, and know which parts of the picture are measured, estimated or unknown.

3 · What is new here

Room visualisation for items that have no 3D model

Room visualisers exist, but they work on the retailer's own catalogue, where every product already has a professionally made 3D model. A Marketplace listing has two or three phone photos and maybe a line of text.

What existsWhat it doesWhy it doesn't solve this problem
IKEA KreativScans a room and lets you place IKEA productsCatalogue items only; can't take a secondhand listing.
Wayfair / Amazon "View in Room" (AR)Places a product's 3D model in your camera viewNeeds a 3D model the retailer made; only for their own products.

Floor-plan tools (Planner 5D, RoomSketcher)Draw a room and add generic furnitureYou draw everything by hand; the piece you're buying is replaced by a generic block.
ChatGPT / image generators"Put this chair in my room" image editsThey redraw both item and room: colours, proportions and details change, and the scale has no basis. The buyer can't trust what they're looking at.

What Theia does differently

  • Works on any listing. It reads public Marketplace metadata and seller dimensions, or a screenshot or image URL. No catalogue model is needed.
  • Keeps the item faithful. Theia only removes background. Every item pixel is the seller's own, and one click switches back to the untouched photo. Sides no photo shows are drawn as a grey silhouette, not generated.
  • Builds 3D from one ordinary photo. It recovers the camera height, tilt and lens, the floor plane and the walls from a single room photo, so items stand upright, sit flush against walls and shrink correctly with distance. You can orbit, pan and zoom around them.
  • Shows its evidence. Every number says where it came from: seller-stated, category estimate, EXIF lens, clicked floor corners, or an assumed phone height.

Honest caveat: we compared against these products from their public descriptions and our own use, not a formal benchmark. Some of them do render better when a catalogue 3D model exists. Theia's advantage is coverage of one-off secondhand items and trustworthiness, not visual polish.

4 · The agents

One narrow language agent and four deterministic tools

We deliberately use the language model for only one job: reading messy listing text and deciding what kind of object it is and how it sits (upright, flat like a rug, or on a wall). Everything that has to be exact (dimensions, pixels, geometry) is done by deterministic, testable code. The LLM can't overwrite any of it.

ComponentWhat it doesTools / inputs it may use
Listing agent (listing_agent.py)Classifies the item (bench, mirror, rug…), picks a pose and raises warnings ("description truncated", "ambiguous item").Only the delimited listing title (≤1,000 chars) and description (≤6,000 chars). No browsing, no image access, no write actions. Output is validated JSON.
Metadata + dimension parserReads Open Graph, JSON-LD and Marketplace bootstrap data; turns "24 inch w / 24.5 h / Seat height 18" or "36 CMT" into metres.Public page HTML via one GET request; never logs in.
Faithful cutout tool (item_cutout.py)Removes the background from up to 5 listing photos at 1600 px, keeping soft edges; restores pale upholstery; clears wall visible between spindles and legs.Best local segmentation model available (BiRefNet → ISNet → U²-Net), else GrabCut.
Room geometry tool (room_geometry.py)Recovers the camera from one photo and builds a metric room box (§4.2).EXIF lens, clicked floor corners, vertical edges, room measurement.
Depth tool (depth_model.py, optional)Turns the room photo into a 3D relief so existing furniture stands up and hides items behind it.Depth Anything V2 Small (ONNX, CPU), aligned to the floor plane.

Model choice

gpt-6-luna via Portkey (Responses API). The job is short classification of untrusted text, so a small, cheap, fast model is enough. It's also the only model allowed on the course key. Early development runs in our log used gpt-6-astra and gpt-6-sol; we switched to gpt-6-luna and made the model an environment setting (THEIA_MODEL). Vision and geometry deliberately don't use an LLM. They run locally, cost no tokens and can't invent details.

4.2 How one photo becomes a 3D room

  1. Lens. The focal length comes from EXIF (35 mm-equivalent). If that's missing, it comes from the clicked corners: a rectangle's two vanishing directions must be perpendicular. If that fails too, we assume a typical 26 mm phone lens.
  2. Floor and camera. With four clicked corners we solve a homography from the floor rectangle to the image, which gives the floor's orientation relative to the camera. Without clicks, we find straight vertical edges (door frames, wall corners, furniture legs), robustly fit their common vanishing point (RANSAC), and get the camera's tilt and roll from it.
  3. Scale. Your confirmed room width if you entered one; otherwise a 1.45 m phone height. The app always tells you which it used.
  4. Room box. The far edge of the clicked floor is the back wall and the sides are the side walls, at the ceiling height you set. The photo is projected back onto these surfaces, so the opening view reproduces the original photo exactly.
  5. Depth (optional). A local depth network's relative depth is calibrated to metres using floor pixels whose true distance we know from step 2.
{{img:docs/figures/geometry_recovery.png|Test on a computer-generated room with a known camera: from the four orange clicks Theia recovers the camera height (1.40 m), tilt (−12.0°) and roll (1.5°) exactly. It then places a 1.2 m bench flush against the back wall and a 0.9 m chair against the right wall, in correct perspective. Before this work, items were flat cutouts pasted at a fixed screen position and resized by a rough depth rule.}}

At scale, model tokens are not the main cost. Hosting and storage are: a small always-on web instance plus CPU time for cutouts and depth inference, which run locally rather than through a paid vision API.

What we did to keep cost down

  • The LLM does one narrow job. It doesn't see images, extract dimensions or reason about geometry. Image-to-3D generators or vision models would cost far more per item and would also invent details.
  • Input caps: title ≤1,000 chars and description ≤6,000 chars (tested: a 20,147-character description is cut to 6,000).
  • Result cache: re-adding the same listing costs 0 tokens (tested: 5 identical adds give 1 model call). Our own log shows why this matters: of the 13 calls, the same bench listing was classified 10 times, with identical token counts and outputs. With the cache, about 8 of those 13 calls would have been free.
  • Hourly cap (default 60 calls/hour) bounds runaway loops (see §6).
  • Zero-token fallback when there is no key, a network error, or the cap is reached.

Did cost cutting hurt quality?

For classification, not in our runs: the same bench listing came back as "bench / upright" whether the model saw a 119-token input or the full 625-token one. The cost cut that did hurt was the old 800 px image cap, which was originally there to keep browser session storage small. It visibly erased detail from items, which was this project's main user complaint. We fixed it by moving images to a server-side cache, so photos are now kept at 1600 px (§6, §7).

7 · Impact

From a 15-minute photo edit to a 2-minute, trustworthy preview

Quality: the item now looks like the item

The most important problem we fixed is the one users noticed first: the old cutout pipeline stripped detail out of items. It downscaled photos twice to 800 px, forced soft edges to hard on/off, painted the wall into the gaps between spindles with a gap-filling filter, and its fallback erased thin legs. The new pipeline keeps 1600 px and soft edges, restores pale upholstery, and clears only regions that match the wall colour at the same height in the photo.

{{img:docs/figures/cutout_before_after.png|Same test photo through both pipelines. Before: wall visible between the spindles and under the seat is kept as part of the bench. What this means at scale

If Theia saves a buyer even one wrong pick-up per apartment hunt, that's roughly an hour of driving and carrying plus a resale loss, and it costs about a cent in model tokens per user per month. The bigger value is less measurable: buyers can act faster on good listings because they no longer have to wait for the seller to reply with measurements.

The time and pick-up figures are estimates from our own use and the user test above, not a controlled study.

8 · Limits and next steps

Where the prototype stops

  • One photo isn't a 3D scan. Without the depth model, existing furniture in the photo is painted flat onto the floor and walls when you orbit far from the photo viewpoint. Without floor clicks, the layout is "rough" and the scale assumes a 1.45 m phone height.
  • Items are photo cards, not meshes. They look right from the angles the seller photographed, and show a silhouette elsewhere. That's honest, but not full 3D.
  • Cutouts can still fail when the item is the same colour as the wall behind it (for example a white table against a white wall). The original-photo toggle is the escape hatch.
  • Stitched panoramas are not a single pinhole camera, so 3D from a panorama is approximate. One photo works best.
  • Marketplace pages can be login-gated; then the user supplies a screenshot or image URL.
  • three.js loads from a CDN, so the 3D view needs internet; offline, the 2D preview is used.

With another month

  1. Phone depth capture (Apple RoomPlan or LiDAR) for a measured room. The iOS scaffolding is already in the repo.
  2. A cutout touch-up brush with undo, and automatic angle tagging of gallery photos.
  3. Real occlusion and lighting matching, so placed items take on the room's light.
  4. A browser extension "Send to Theia" button on Marketplace pages.
  5. A five-person study measuring time-to-decision, wrong-pick-up rate and trust ratings against the photo-editor baseline.
Boundary: Theia helps decide whether an item is worth the trip. It doesn't replace a tape measure or confirming with the seller.
